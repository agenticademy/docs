# Privacy, bias and owning the result

AI principles · Chapter 7 of 8 · 12 minutes

## Goals

## The read

### What you paste may be kept

When you paste text into an online AI chat assistant, it goes to the company that runs the service. Depending on the service, your plan and your settings, it may be stored, and it may be used to train later models. Before you paste anything private, look in the service's settings and terms for what it does with your chats.

### Share only what the task needs

Personal data is anything that points to a person: a name, an email address, a phone number, a home address, an order or ID number. Most tasks do not need it. A summary of a complaint works just as well with “[Name 1]” in place of the customer's name.

Sharing only what a task needs is called data minimisation. Taking each personal detail out before you paste, or swapping it for a placeholder, is called redacting. A tool can find many details for you, but it misses some, so read the text yourself too.

### Bias comes from data and from design

Bias here means that a system's outputs lean one way, for or against some groups of people. It can come from skewed data, as chapter 1 showed. A system trained on past hiring decisions can learn to favour the kind of people hired before. It can also come from design, the choices its builders make about what to include, test and leave out. Either way, the harm falls on some groups more than others, often those who were rare in the data or missing from the tests. When an output is about people, ask who it might treat worse.

### Say when AI helped, and own the result

A disclosure line says what AI did and how you checked it, such as “AI translated this letter. A colleague who reads both languages checked it.” Some schools, employers and publishers ask for one. It does not hand over the responsibility: what you send or publish is yours, whoever wrote the first draft.

## Check

### Question 1 of 4

You want an AI chat assistant to sum up a customer's complaint. It holds the customer's name, email and order number. What should you do first?

- Paste it as it is: the assistant needs every detail.
- **✓** Replace the name, email and order number with placeholders, then paste it.
- Paste it, then ask the assistant to forget the personal details.

**Why:** A summary works just as well with placeholders, so take the details out before the text leaves your hands. The assistant does not need them to sum up the complaint, and asking it to forget does not undo what was already sent.

### Question 2 of 4

Where do you find out whether what you paste into an AI service may be stored or used to train later models?

- **✓** In the service's settings and terms, which can differ between services and plans.
- Nowhere: every AI service keeps everything you type.
- In the assistant's own answer, if you ask it.

**Why:** Each service sets its own rules, and a free plan and a paid plan can differ; its settings and terms say what applies to you. Not every service keeps everything. And as chapter 3 showed, an assistant can state wrong things with confidence, so its answer about its own service proves nothing.

### Question 3 of 4

A company trains a system to rank job applications on ten years of its own hiring decisions. Most people it hired in those years were men. What is the likely problem?

- None: a program has no opinions, so it ranks everyone fairly.
- **✓** It may rank people like past hires higher, so women lose out, though nobody told it to.
- It will be slower than a person who reads every application.

**Why:** A system learns the patterns in its data, including who was hired before, so it can carry an old imbalance into new decisions. Having no opinions does not stop that, and the harm falls on the group that was rare in the data. Speed is not the problem here.

### Question 4 of 4

AI wrote the first draft of your club's newsletter. You sent it out, and it gave the wrong date for the next meeting. Which is true?

- The mistake is the AI's, because it wrote the date.
- A line saying AI helped would have made it nobody's fault.
- **✓** The mistake is yours: you chose to send it, so checking the date was your job.

**Why:** What you send or publish is yours, whoever wrote the first draft. A disclosure line tells readers how the text was made; it does not hand over the responsibility. Checking names, numbers and dates before you send is the check that would have caught it.

## What you can do now

- Take names, emails, phone numbers, addresses and ID numbers out of a text before you paste it, and check what a tool missed.
- Explain how bias can come from the data a system learned from and from how it was built, and who it can harm.
- Write a line that says what AI did and how you checked it.
- Treat what you send or publish as yours, whoever wrote the first draft.
